# Copilot Lab — helping a support rep decide what to do next

A customer says something is broken. That tells a support rep where to start, not what caused it.

I built this experiment to explore how AI could help a rep move from that first complaint to a justified next action. It should remember what has been learned, ask useful questions, inspect available product information and change its advice when the evidence changes.

The rep owns the conversation and the decisions. Copilot should reduce the work of investigating, not become another thing the rep has to manage.

## The support judgment I want it to preserve

- When a complaint is vague, ask a question that separates plausible problems.
- Use account information already available instead of asking the customer to repeat it.
- Use the rep’s answer to choose the next useful check. Do not restart the interview.
- Separate what the customer reported, what product records show and what remains a possible explanation.
- Change direction when new evidence contradicts the current explanation.
- Suggest a supported fix, then wait for its result. Suggesting a fix does not resolve a case.
- When engineering is needed, explain why, preserve what was learned and specify what engineering should investigate.
- Avoid repeating advice when nothing has changed. Leave the rep in control of confirmation, closure and handoff.

These are the behaviors being examined, not a claim that every conversation will exhibit them reliably.

## The experiment

This is a small **scenario-based evaluation using simulated support data**, followed by a **formative owner walkthrough**. Its purpose is to examine the behavior and improve the design. It is not a randomized or comparative effectiveness study.

The question is: **can an assistant help a rep reach the next justified action without adding unnecessary questions or work?**

Two scenarios begin with the same complaint: “Our workspace stopped working after the migration.” Copilot initially knows only that complaint and basic account information: the workspace, active account, migration timing and availability of an administrator. It can consult the supplied support policy.

The person trying the experiment has a separate customer-facts sheet. Those observations are not automatically sent to the model. The rep answers Copilot in their own words. Supplying the relevant request identifiers makes the corresponding simulated product lookup available; the model must request it before receiving the records. Choosing a scenario in the interface does not tell the model its intended ending.

The scenarios, account details, product records and support policy are authored for this experiment. They are not real customer incidents. The advice is generated by the configured model, `gpt-5.6-luna`; it is not a prerecorded response.

### Scenario 1: investigation to resolution

Copilot asks what failed. The rep explains who is affected, what they attempted, successful comparisons and the affected request IDs. Copilot inspects the request records and permitted procedure. An older denied permission was used even though the current permission allows access. It proposes an authorized administrator refresh cached access, then waits for the customer’s result.

The rep confirms the original activity works and separately closes the case.

### Scenario 2: investigation to engineering escalation

The initial investigation supports the same fix. This time, the rep reports that the retry still fails, now with a blank page, and supplies new request IDs.

Copilot requests the retry records. Access is now allowed, but rendering returns a server error. It changes direction and prepares an engineering request containing the affected workspace and users, the previous check, the retry identifiers, what changed and the logs engineering should inspect. The underlying server-error cause remains unknown.

The rep can review and accept the handoff. Acceptance records a decision to transfer; it does not send the draft or claim customer recovery.

## What happened when I tried it

On 16 September, I walked through the investigation and explored both outcomes in one continuing case.

| Information I provided | What Copilot did |
| --- | --- |
| A vague complaint after migration | Asked which activity failed and what the customer saw, rather than assuming migration was the cause. |
| Three affected members, successful comparisons and request IDs | Requested access records and support policy, then proposed the supported administrator action. |
| All three could open the workspace again | Recognized reported recovery and said further troubleshooting was unnecessary unless the issue returned. |
| A later blank-page report and new request IDs | Requested retry records, distinguished successful permission checks from a rendering failure, and prepared engineering investigation. |

My immediate feedback was that it seemed to be working. The saved walkthrough contains seven model calls and three product/reference reads. It contains no explicit Close or Accept handoff action; those controls were exercised separately in developer-run checks. The session remained open. This distinction matters: my walkthrough demonstrated the changing advice, not two independently completed customer cases.

## How I checked it

- **Software checks:** 86 automated checks passed when the two scenarios were implemented. These include keeping private records out of the opening input, requiring identifiers before a lookup is available, and keeping the selected ending out of the model input.
- **End-to-end scenario checks:** both paths were exercised with real model responses through the application and browser controls. One ended in explicit rep-confirmed closure; the other in an accepted, unsent handoff after a failed fix.
- **Formative owner walkthrough:** I entered the simulated customer observations and assessed the responses. The session showed useful evidence-based changes of direction, with the limits recorded above.

These are different kinds of evidence. Passing software checks does not measure support quality. My own walkthrough is useful design feedback, not independent participant research. There is no current measurement of time saved, fewer questions, reduced workload or superiority over a simpler assistant. The two scenarios do not establish reliability across long or unfamiliar cases.

## Why LTS and human-centered AI influenced this

[LTS-VoiceAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19952) separates maintaining an understanding from deciding when to respond. My support adaptation is to keep updating the investigation and offer advice when information changes what the rep should do next.

This implementation works from submitted text updates and retained case information. It is LTS-inspired, not a reproduction of the paper’s streaming architecture.

The human-centered question is practical: does the assistant support the rep’s judgment while preserving their ability to correct it and decide what happens? Having confirmation buttons alone does not answer that. The advice must also be worth reading and checking.

## What changed during the experiment

Earlier versions repeated questions, discarded checked evidence when a rep added a finding, and did not provide a clear stopping point for handoff. New findings now preserve the current evidence; explicit scope corrections retire the old snapshot. The rep can accept a handoff without pretending the customer recovered.

An earlier evaluation supplied too much case information upfront. Its polished answers did not establish investigation ability. I withdrew that interpretation and tested incomplete reports with evidence revealed only after lookup. Those historical results remain separate from the current scenario walkthrough.

Some weaknesses remain. Free-text reports of recovery are not the same as pressing the confirmation and closure controls. Scope correction currently retires the whole evidence snapshot. Earlier outputs also included an unsupported wording change from records being “missing” to being “excluded”; the current access scenarios do not establish that this issue is solved everywhere.

## Try it

Open the local webpage and choose **Investigate and resolve** or **Investigate and escalate**. Click **Get advice**. Expand **Customer facts for this practice** for the simulated observations you can provide when asked. Phrase them naturally; do not tell the assistant which ending to choose.

Use the outcome buttons to confirm success and close, or report the failed retry as a new finding and review the engineering handoff. Your submitted text goes to OpenAI, so use simulated details. Handoffs remain local drafts.

The bounded experiment is complete enough to describe and inspect. It demonstrates useful behavior in these scenarios; it does not establish a generally reliable support assistant.

## Method references

[Qualitative and quantitative usability testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/quant-vs-qual/) explains the distinction between finding design issues and measuring performance. [Microsoft’s scenario-based agent evaluation example](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/ise/intuitive-evaluation-framework-for-agentic-chatbots/) shows how simulated support interactions can exercise agent behavior. These inform the terminology; this small owner walkthrough is not claimed to reproduce their methods or results.
